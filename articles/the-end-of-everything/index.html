The End of Everything

“For eternity a minute is equal to a century, for infinity the atom is equal to the nebula. But I am floating neither in infinity nor in eternity; I am situated in a world that my presence defines.”
— Simone de Beauvoir

Part 1: Apocalypse

The Fire and The Comet

The Fire

Humphrey Smith, a Quaker priest routinely arrested for his non-Protestant teachings, had a vision of an apocalyptic scene. He saw God’s fury let loose in the city of London, punishing it for its sins. “The fire consumed all foundations which the City stood upon,” he wrote. “For the burning was of God, and could never be quenched nor overcome.” Humphrey knew that the foundations of his world were not permanent. They can and will be destroyed in due time. In the decade prior, the people of England saw the end of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms and with it the execution of King Charles I — the first break England had from the monarchy. It did not last long. The same year Humphrey made his prediction of a fire in London, King Charles II reclaimed the crown his father lost. The world felt unstable. Many feared what was ahead of them, and rightfully so.

A comet would fly over London five years later — a dark omen warning all willing to listen. With it, the comet brought plague to the city. Over the course of the following year, the Great Plague would go on to kill a quarter of the city’s population, and still residents believed the worst was to come. For many, it was. There was fear in the streets that pushed many into church pews. Next year there would not just be plague but war and, as Humphrey Smith predicted, fire. The same year as Humphrey’s vision, a writer predicted that the “Egge of Antichristianisme” would hatch. The writer only signed “Friend of the Truth” but their prediction was less ambiguous. The world was about to end, and it would happen in the year of the Devil: 1666.

The growing plague, the comet, the war, the fall and rise of the monarchy — all coalescing around the number of the Beast. People were scared and with good reason. If the end was coming, it would happen now. Even the recently crowned king would flee the City of London in fear. Outside the city, a farmer was unsettled while collecting his hen’s eggs. One egg, freshly laid by his hen, had the words “Christ is Coming” inscribed on it. He watched his prophet hen as it laid another egg with another warning. Crowds formed to see the prophetic eggs for themselves. Townsfolk began wearing seals with Christ’s initials in hopes of securing a place in heaven. The “Egge of Antichristianisme” prediction had come true.

Still in the city, Thomas Farriner noticed a small fire in his baker’s shop on Pudding Lane. Thomas believed he put the fire out when he went to sleep. In the middle of the night, it consumed his home. It then grew into the neighboring houses, then neighboring streets, eventually reaching the River Thames in the south, devastating everything in its path. Thousands of homes were devoured. The fire spilled outside of the western portion of the London Wall. It burned for days. The city went up in “a most horrid malicious bloody flame, not like the fine flame of an ordinary fire,” as Samuel Pepys recalled in his journal. The vision and the prediction were correct. Plague, war, and flames worked together to collapse the city. The world was ending.

The Comet

A few hundred years after a comet beaconed the apocalypse in London, another comet would spell the end for the rest of the world. Camille Flammarion published an article in a French newspaper warning of the impending arrival of Halley’s Comet. Humankind would be given one last beautiful show and then “the human race would perish in a paroxysm of joy and delirium.” Once in a human lifetime, Halley’s Comet pays the world a visit. After this one, thought the masses, there will not be a next human lifetime for it to return to. Unlike previous passes, this time it was “going to wrap its tail around the earth,” strangling it.

It was discovered that the tail contained a deadly cyanogen gas. “A grain of its potassium salt touched to the tongue is enough to cause instant death,” Camille said, “all human life on our planet will then be snuffed out.” Another French astronomer speculated on the effects of the tail’s X-rays, suggesting it could cause all the atmosphere’s water to condense at once, leading to worldwide floods reminiscent of the biblical cleansing. In the Americas, the New York Times published an article headlined “Comet’s Poisonous Tail.” In Korea, a former high-ranking government official fled the country, believing the comet was a sign foretelling the downfall of the imperial dynasty.

Panic spread wide and ran deep. People prepared for the end in every way they could. Some threw parties, others prepared for the worst. Creditors stopped paying bank loans; German farmers didn’t plant their crops that year; some American farmers sold their land. Many turned to curative pills and elixirs that promised to negate the effects of the tail. People tried sealing off their homes, covering the keyholes the toxic gas might seep through. A teenager in Puerto Rico, Fernando Colón-Vásquez, hiked miles from home to a cave and crawled deep inside it to escape whatever the comet might bring. Outside Atlanta, three hundred people sought refuge in a cave and prayed through the night, expecting morning never to come.

Reports of suicide emerged from multiple countries. France experienced flooding around the Seine that many took as confirmation. “This was the way it was in the scriptures,” one witness, eight years old at the time, later recalled, “they all talked about the world coming to an end.”

Part 2: Dilemmas

The King and the Student

“We are all alone, born alone, die alone,” wrote Hunter S. Thompson, “and—in spite of True Romance magazines—we shall all someday look back on our lives and see that, in spite of our company, we were alone the whole way.” Eight years after those words were published, Thompson took his own life. No great fire. No poisonous comet. Just a past tense: “Hunter.”

Yale professor Shelly Kagan has criticized the cliche “everyone dies alone” in his lecture series on death, arguing the phrase says little that’s useful — it’s also true, and trivial, that everyone eats their lunch alone. What matters more is the “born alone” half of the phrase: the importance of death is the life just before it. Death doesn’t give life meaning — life gives death meaning. Perhaps “everyone dies alone” is really gesturing at “everyone lives alone,” a fact we only notice looking back.

The King

In the Theravāda tradition, there’s a teaching called The Simile of the Mountains, where the Buddha sits with a great king and proposes a scenario: reliable messengers arrive one by one from each direction, each reporting an enormous mountain, crushing everything in its path, closing in on the king’s land. There is no escaping it. Asked what he would do, the king says there isn’t much to do but right conduct, skillful deeds, and supporting those around him. The Buddha then points out that these mountains — aging, sickness, and death — are already closing in on him and everyone he rules, and asks again what he will do.

The Student

Jean-Paul Sartre tells a story about a moral dilemma one of his students faced. The student’s older brother had died in battle; his mother lived alone, and he was her one consolation. He had to decide whether to stay and care for her, or leave to join the Free French Forces and avenge his brother — one path concrete and immediate but limited to a single person, the other aimed at something larger but uncertain and easily frustrated. No doctrine could resolve it for him cleanly; even choosing which priest to consult would already be a choice. In the end, the student told Sartre that feeling was what counted — that he should choose whichever direction he felt most pulled toward. Sartre’s answer to him was simply: you are free, therefore choose — that is, invent.

Sartre never tells his audience what the student ultimately chose. That omission is the point: only the individual can decide what is right for them. The Buddha’s parable asks what you’d do facing the end of everything you know; Sartre’s story asks the next question — given that you know how much your choices matter, how do you choose at all? His answer offers no relief, only freedom.

Part 3: Aftermath

We don’t know whether Sartre’s student joined the military. We do know that Fernando Colón-Vásquez, the boy who hid in the cave, did. Halley’s Comet passed in 1910 without incident; the toxic tail never touched the earth at all. Fernando left the cave, grew up, and eventually worked in insurance after a stint in the military. That’s nearly everything we know about him. He died in 1950, at 54 — not by comet, not by fire, just alone, the way everyone eventually is. As T.S. Eliot put it, this is how the world ends: not with a bang but a whimper.

The Londoners of 1666 are gone too, of course. The fire itself killed remarkably few people by name — a maid, a shoemaker, one other recorded death in a church — but it left tens of thousands homeless, and among the uncounted dead were a poet and his wife who froze to death in a refugee camp after fleeing the flames.

Part 4: Consultations

The world we knew as children is already gone; it left with the people who shared it with us. Perhaps the world ends this way instead — not all at once, but one departure at a time, a billion small worlds flickering out until there’s no more light. The mountains are always closing in. But that is exactly what allows meaning to exist at all: we don’t discover it, we invent it. If it were handed to us freely, we might not recognize its weight.

The world might not end. Yours will. You don’t need to live through an apocalypse to find purpose — the purpose only ever comes from you, wherever you choose to look for it. As Annie Dillard put it, how we spend our days is how we spend our lives. The universe offers no meaning by default. You find it anyway — because the stars only align when you go looking for constellations.
